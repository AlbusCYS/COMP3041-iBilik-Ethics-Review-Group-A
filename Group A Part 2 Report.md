# 1. Introduction

Digital rental platforms play an increasingly important role in connecting prospective tenants with accommodation providers. However, their design decisions extend beyond facilitating transactions, as they can influence housing accessibility, user safety, privacy and financial fairness. This report examines iBilik, a Malaysian accommodation platform that supports room and property searches, alongside iBilikPlus, which provides tenancy-management and payment-related services.

Building upon the preliminary investigation conducted in Part 1, this report evaluates four interconnected ethical issues: discrimination and unequal access to housing; safety, trust and platform responsibility; privacy, profiling and personal-data use; and financial fairness, transparency and accountability. The initial investigation involved platform walkthroughs, policy analysis, technical inspection and publicly available evidence, identifying concerns regarding demographic search filters, verification practices, personal-data processing and financial conditions.

The present investigation builds on these findings through further evidence collection, stakeholder analysis and application of the Software Engineering Code of Ethics and Professional Practice (ACM/IEEE-CS Joint Task Force, 1999). Where professional guidelines are insufficient to resolve competing ethical interests, utilitarianism and deontology are applied to examine the consequences and moral obligations associated with particular design decisions.

Rather than treating each concern independently, the report considers how platform functionality, business objectives and professional responsibilities interact. This approach supports a more balanced ethical assessment and the development of practical recommendations grounded in both evidence and ethical reasoning.

# 6. Comparative Application of Ethical Theories

## 6.1 Limitations of the Software Engineering Code of Ethics

The Software Engineering Code of Ethics provides a professional foundation for evaluating the responsibilities of software engineers towards users and wider society. Nevertheless, applying its principles to iBilik reveals ethical dilemmas that cannot necessarily be resolved through professional guidelines alone.

A particularly relevant example concerns demographic preferences in accommodation listings. The Part 1 investigation identified structured filters relating to race, nationality and gender, alongside listings expressing preferences for particular demographic groups. These features raise concerns about equal access to housing, particularly where platform-supported classifications may facilitate discriminatory outcomes.

The SE Code supports consideration of the public good and equitable access, particularly through Clauses 1.02 and 1.07, as identified in Part 1. However, restricting all demographic preferences could also interfere with legitimate concerns regarding personal safety, privacy and autonomy in shared accommodation.

Consequently, the Code provides important professional responsibilities without necessarily establishing how competing interests should be prioritised in every context. A comparison of utilitarianism and deontology offers a means of examining this dilemma more critically.

## 6.2 Utilitarian Perspective

Utilitarianism evaluates actions according to their consequences, generally favouring decisions that produce the greatest overall well-being while minimising harm.

From this perspective, iBilik's demographic filters should be evaluated according to their effects on tenants, landlords and society. Certain preferences, such as gender-related requirements in shared living arrangements, may provide benefits relating to personal comfort, privacy and perceived safety. Removing these preferences indiscriminately could therefore impose disadvantages on some users.

However, the same reasoning does not automatically justify racial or nationality-based exclusion. The Part 1 investigation found substantial differences in visible listing availability when race filters were applied. Such features may reduce housing opportunities for particular groups, increase search difficulties and contribute to broader social exclusion.

A utilitarian assessment would therefore favour distinguishing between preferences supported by proportionate safety or privacy considerations and restrictions that generate substantial exclusion without comparable benefits. Nevertheless, this conclusion depends on the actual consequences of these features, which would require further empirical investigation.

## 6.3 Deontological Perspective

Deontology evaluates actions according to moral duties and principles rather than their consequences alone. Fundamental considerations include fairness, respect for individuals and the avoidance of treating people merely as instruments for achieving another party's goals.

Applied to iBilik, a deontological approach raises objections to excluding prospective tenants primarily because of race or nationality. Even where discriminatory preferences benefit individual landlords or simplify accommodation matching, such benefits do not necessarily justify unequal treatment.

Unlike a purely consequence-based approach, deontology places greater emphasis on whether the design itself respects users as individuals deserving equal moral consideration.

Nevertheless, gender-related preferences in shared accommodation introduce competing rights and duties. Respecting a tenant's equal standing must be considered alongside an existing occupant's legitimate interests in privacy and personal autonomy. Accordingly, deontological reasoning does not automatically require identical treatment of every demographic preference.

## 6.4 Comparison and Ethical Judgement

Both approaches raise concerns about iBilik's demographic filtering features, but they reach their conclusions through different reasoning.

Utilitarianism focuses on the balance between potential benefits and harms. It may support limited demographic preferences where demonstrable safety or privacy benefits outweigh the resulting restrictions. Deontology places stronger emphasis on principled limits to discriminatory treatment, even where exclusion could benefit particular users.

The distinction becomes particularly significant when considering race-based restrictions. A utilitarian approach could theoretically permit a restriction if sufficient benefits were demonstrated, whereas a deontological approach would challenge whether race-based exclusion is morally acceptable in the first place.

In our assessment, deontology provides the stronger foundation for evaluating racial exclusion because equal moral consideration should not depend solely on aggregate benefits. However, utilitarian reasoning remains useful when assessing practical safeguards intended to address legitimate privacy and safety concerns.

The resulting ethical position is that iBilik should reconsider platform-supported race-based exclusion while adopting more narrowly defined, transparent and proportionate approaches to legitimate shared-accommodation requirements. This combines principled respect for equal treatment with consideration of real-world consequences.

# 7. Overall Critical Reflection

The investigation demonstrates that evaluating a software platform ethically requires more than determining whether particular features are technically functional or contractually disclosed. Features that appear neutral from an engineering perspective may influence users' opportunities, expectations and ability to make informed decisions.

A significant insight concerns the distinction between platform responsibility and individual user behaviour. Although landlords, advertisers and tenants make independent decisions, the platform determines which preferences can be expressed, how verification indicators are presented and what information users receive before making commitments. These design choices influence how users interact with one another and cannot be separated entirely from the resulting ethical consequences.

Applying the SE Code also highlights the limitations of relying exclusively on professional standards. While the Code establishes important expectations concerning fairness, safety, privacy and honesty, practical decisions may involve competing obligations. Strengthening identity verification, for example, could improve trust while introducing additional personal-data collection and privacy risks.

The comparison between utilitarianism and deontology further demonstrates that ethical evaluation depends on both outcomes and underlying principles. Consequentialist reasoning provides a useful basis for assessing practical benefits and harms, while deontological reasoning helps establish boundaries that should not be disregarded merely for convenience or commercial advantage.

Our understanding of professional responsibility has therefore developed beyond identifying potentially harmful features. Software engineers must also anticipate foreseeable consequences, recognise competing stakeholder interests and critically evaluate whether proposed safeguards introduce additional ethical problems.

Importantly, our findings remain limited by the scope of the investigation. Observed platform features and sampled listings demonstrate potential concerns but do not necessarily establish the prevalence of unethical practices throughout the platform. Maintaining this distinction is essential to producing defensible ethical judgements.

# 8. Integrated Recommendations and Conclusion

The four ethical issues suggest that iBilik's ethical standing could be improved through a combination of design changes, stronger user protections and more transparent governance.

First, the platform should review demographic filtering options and distinguish legitimate accommodation requirements from potentially discriminatory exclusions. Second, verification indicators should communicate precisely what has been verified, supported by clearer warnings and reporting mechanisms. Third, personal-data processing should follow data-minimisation principles and provide users with more meaningful information and control over their data. Finally, fees, refund restrictions and changes to financial terms should be communicated clearly before users make commitments.

These recommendations reflect both the SE Code's emphasis on professional responsibility and the wider ethical requirement to balance beneficial outcomes with respect for individual rights. Their implementation would need to consider feasibility, stakeholder interests and potential unintended consequences.

Overall, the investigation highlights that ethical responsibility in software engineering extends beyond legal compliance and technical reliability. Although iBilik provides a useful service connecting accommodation seekers with providers, its design and operational practices raise important questions about fairness, trust, privacy and financial transparency.

By integrating first-hand evidence, professional ethical standards and broader ethical theories, this report demonstrates the importance of evaluating software systems not only according to what they enable users to do, but also according to how those capabilities affect the people who depend on them.
